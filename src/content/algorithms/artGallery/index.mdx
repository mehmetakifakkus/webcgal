An art gallery wants to watch every point of its floor with cameras. The floor plan is a *simple polygon* *P* with *n* vertices, and a camera is a point in *P* that turns all the way round: it *sees* a point *q* of *P* when the segment from the camera to *q* lies in *P* (it may run along a wall, but it may not leave the room). How many cameras does the gallery need, and where should they go? Finding the smallest number for a given polygon is NP-hard, so the question becomes how many cameras are enough for *every* polygon with *n* vertices.

This page follows section 3.1, *Guarding and Triangulations*, of Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications, 3rd ed., by de Berg et al.

**Chvátal's art gallery theorem.** For a simple polygon with *n* vertices, ⌊*n*/3⌋ cameras are always sufficient, and occasionally necessary, to see every point of it. They are necessary for a *comb*: a base with *k* narrow prongs has 3*k* vertices, and the points that see the tip of a prong form a narrow wedge below it; for narrow prongs the wedges of two different tips do not meet, so no camera watches two tips and *k* = *n*/3 cameras are needed. The *Comb* preset has 12 vertices and 4 prongs; no 3 of its corners see the whole room.

**Fisk's proof.** First triangulate *P*: every simple polygon can be split by *n* − 3 diagonals into *n* − 2 triangles (see [Polygon Triangulation](/algorithms/triangulation) for how). A camera at a vertex of a triangle sees the whole triangle, which is convex, so it is enough to choose vertices that touch every triangle. Fisk's idea is to *3-colour* the vertices, so that the three vertices of every triangle get three different colours. Such a colouring always exists, because of the *dual tree*: it has a node for every triangle and an arc between two triangles that share a diagonal. Removing a diagonal cuts the polygon in two, so removing an arc cuts the dual graph in two, and the dual graph is a tree. A depth-first walk of the tree colours the vertices: the first triangle gets three different colours, and every further triangle shares a diagonal with its parent, so two of its vertices are already coloured and the third, which no earlier triangle has reached, gets the colour that is missing. Now every triangle has a vertex of each colour. The three colour classes together hold *n* vertices, so the smallest one has at most ⌊*n*/3⌋, and cameras at its vertices touch every triangle and see all of *P*.

**Running time.** The triangulation takes O(*n* log *n*) time with the algorithm of chapter 3; the dual tree, the depth-first walk and the counting of the colours take O(*n*). So a set of at most ⌊*n*/3⌋ cameras that sees a simple polygon with *n* vertices can be found in O(*n* log *n*) time.

**The bound is not the best for every room.** ⌊*n*/3⌋ is what the worst polygon needs, and Fisk's construction never uses more, but it may use more than a room needs. In the *Convex room* preset, a regular polygon with 9 vertices, it places 3 cameras, while a single camera at any corner sees the whole room; the *L-shaped gallery* gets 2 cameras, while one at its inner corner *v*<sub>4</sub> sees both wings. The camera mode is there to try to beat Fisk.

Step through the construction with the player. The room is drawn in black with its corners *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, …; the diagonals of the triangulation are grey and the dual tree a dashed grey line between the triangles' centres. The triangle in focus is orange, coloured vertices are filled red, green or blue and uncoloured ones are hollow; the smallest colour class is ringed, then its vertices get cameras (squares) and the yellow regions they see. The **Colours** panel lists the vertices of every colour, **Cameras** what share of the room each camera sees, and **Coverage** compares the number of cameras with ⌊*n*/3⌋. The **Mode** menu switches between *Fisk's construction* and *Place your own cameras*. In Fisk's construction, drag the corners to change the room; it must stay simple, so a drag across a wall turns the outline red and jumps back on release. In the camera mode, click a corner to place or remove a camera: the yellow regions are what your cameras see and red is what nobody sees, and the description tells you when the room is covered. The shares are counted on a grid of sample points 5 units apart. **New random input** draws a new star-shaped room. The **Code** tab runs the same construction through `artGallery`, `visibilityPolygon` and `drawArtGallery`; see the [documentation](/documentation) for the rest of the library. For more on the problem, see [wiki/Art_gallery_problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_gallery_problem).
