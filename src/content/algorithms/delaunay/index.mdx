A *triangulation* of a point set *P* is a maximal set of non-crossing edges between points of *P*: it splits the convex hull of *P* into triangles. Among all triangulations of *P*, the *Delaunay triangulation* is the one in which the *circumcircle* of every triangle is empty: no point of *P* lies strictly inside it. It is unique when no four points lie on a common circle; otherwise several triangulations are equally good. Of all triangulations of *P*, it maximizes the minimum angle, so it avoids long, thin triangles. That makes it the standard choice for interpolating terrain heights from sample points and for building meshes.

This page follows chapter 9, *Delaunay Triangulations*, of Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications, 3rd ed., by de Berg et al.

**Legal edges and flips.** An edge *p<sub>i</sub>p<sub>j</sub>* shared by the triangles *p<sub>i</sub>p<sub>j</sub>p<sub>k</sub>* and *p<sub>i</sub>p<sub>j</sub>p<sub>l</sub>* is *illegal* when *p<sub>l</sub>* lies inside the circumcircle of *p<sub>i</sub>p<sub>j</sub>p<sub>k</sub>*. Then the quadrilateral of the two triangles is convex, and *flipping* the edge, replacing it by the other diagonal *p<sub>k</sub>p<sub>l</sub>*, increases the smallest angle. A triangulation with only legal edges is a Delaunay triangulation.

**The randomized incremental algorithm, expected O(n log n).** The points are inserted one at a time in random order. Each new point *p<sub>r</sub>* is located in the triangle that contains it, which is split into three (or, when *p<sub>r</sub>* lies on an edge, the two triangles at that edge are split into four). Only the edges opposite *p<sub>r</sub>* can now be illegal; they go on a *flip stack*. Each edge popped from the stack is tested: an illegal edge is flipped, and the two edges it exposes, again opposite *p<sub>r</sub>*, are pushed. When the stack is empty, all edges are legal again and the next point is inserted. In expectation the whole run makes a linear number of flips.

**The points p₋₁ and p₋₂.** To start with one triangle that contains every point, the algorithm takes *p₀*, the lexicographically highest point (largest *y*, then largest *x*), and two symbolic points *p₋₁* and *p₋₂* far away. They are not placed at real coordinates: very large coordinates would make the in-circle tests numerically fragile, and no finite choice is safe for every input. Instead, every test involving them follows symbolic rules that behave as if they were infinitely far away, so they never change which edges of the real points are legal. At the end they are removed with all their edges, leaving the Delaunay triangulation of *P*. In the player, edges towards *p₋₁* and *p₋₂* are drawn as short dashed rays: up-left towards *p₋₁* and down-right towards *p₋₂*.

**Point location.** The book finds the triangle that contains *p<sub>r</sub>* with a *history DAG*: every triangle ever created is a node, and the triangles that replaced it after a split or a flip are its children, so a search descends from the first triangle to a current one in expected O(log n) time per point. This page keeps the steps simple and uses a plain scan over the current triangles instead; the triangulation it builds is the same.

The Delaunay triangulation is the dual graph of the *Voronoi diagram*: two points are joined by a Delaunay edge exactly when their Voronoi cells share an edge.
