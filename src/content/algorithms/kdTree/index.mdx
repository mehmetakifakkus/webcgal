A database query such as "all employees born between 1960 and 1970 who earn between 3,000 and 4,000 a month" becomes a geometric question when every record is drawn as a point, its birth year as the *x*-coordinate and its salary as the *y*-coordinate: the answer is the set of points in the axis-parallel rectangle [1960 : 1970] × [3000 : 4000]. This is *orthogonal range searching*: preprocess a set *P* of *n* points in the plane so that, for any query rectangle *R* = [*x* : *x′*] × [*y* : *y′*], the points of *P* in *R* can be reported quickly. A *kd-tree* is a data structure for it that uses linear storage.

This page follows section 5.2, *Kd-Trees*, of Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications, 3rd ed., by de Berg et al.

**One dimension first.** On a line, store the points in the leaves of a balanced binary search tree. A query [*x* : *x′*] searches for *x* and for *x′*; the two search paths share a part from the root down to the *split node*, where they part. Every subtree that hangs off the path to *x* below the split node on its right, or off the path to *x′* below the split node on its left, lies completely in the range and is reported whole; only the leaves at the ends of the two paths need a test. That takes O(log *n* + *k*) time for *k* reported points.

**Building the kd-tree.** In the plane, the tree splits on *x* and on *y* in turn. At the root, a vertical line ℓ through the median *x*-coordinate splits *P* into *P*<sub>1</sub>, the points left of or on ℓ, and *P*<sub>2</sub>, the points right of it, so *P*<sub>1</sub> gets ⌈*n*/2⌉ of the points. The two children split their own subsets with a horizontal line through the median *y*-coordinate, their children with a vertical line again, and so on; a set of one point becomes a leaf. Every point ends in one leaf and every split node has two children, so the tree has *n* leaves and *n* − 1 split nodes and needs O(*n*) storage: the ten points of the *Textbook example* give 19 nodes, with depth 4. Its depth is O(log *n*), because the subsets halve at each level. If each median and split takes linear time, the building time satisfies *T*(*n*) = O(*n*) + 2*T*(⌈*n*/2⌉), which solves to O(*n* log *n*). The book gets the linear time by sorting the points on *x* and on *y* once, beforehand, and splitting both sorted lists at every node; the player simply sorts the points of each node, which is fast enough for a few dozen points.

**Regions and the query.** Every node *v* has a *region*, region(*v*): the rectangle bounded by the split lines of its ancestors (in the book it may be unbounded; the player bounds the root's region by the frame of the plane). The region of the left child of *v* is the part of region(*v*) on or left of (or below) the split line of *v*, that of the right child the part on or right of (or above) it, and region(*v*) contains all points stored below *v*. **SearchKdTree** starts at the root. At a leaf it reports the point if it lies in *R*. At a split node it looks at each child in turn: if the child's region lies completely in *R*, **ReportSubtree** reports every leaf below it, in time linear in their number; if the region intersects *R*, the search descends into the child; and if the region is disjoint from *R*, the child is skipped. *R* and the regions are closed, so a point on the boundary of *R* is reported.

**Why the query takes O(√*n* + *k*) time.** Reporting whole subtrees costs O(*k*) in total. Every other node the search visits has a region that intersects *R* without lying in it, so the boundary of *R* crosses that region, and one of the four lines through the sides of *R* crosses it too. It is therefore enough to count the regions a vertical line crosses (horizontal lines are symmetric). A vertical line lies on one side of the root's vertical split line, so it meets only one of the two child regions; that child splits horizontally, and the line can meet both of its children, but from each of those the same argument repeats. With *Q*(*n*) the number of regions crossed in a tree of *n* points whose root splits vertically, *Q*(*n*) = O(1) + 2*Q*(*n*/4), which solves to O(√*n*). So a query visits O(√*n*) nodes besides the reported subtrees and takes O(√*n* + *k*) time. This is a worst-case bound; a small rectangle usually costs far less: the *Empty query* preset visits only 4 of the 19 nodes.

**Equal coordinates.** The description above assumes that no two points share an *x*- or a *y*-coordinate, otherwise a median line might not split the points into two halves. Later in the chapter the book removes this assumption with *composite numbers*: on the *x* axis a point is compared by (*x* | *y*), that is by *x* and then by *y*, on the *y* axis by (*y* | *x*). The player sorts the points of every node in this order (and coincident points by their number), so each split puts ⌈*n*/2⌉ points on the left even when several points lie on the split line; the line stays at the plain coordinate, and a point on it lies in both closed child regions, so no point is missed. The *Square grid 4×4* preset, where four points share every *x* and every *y*, shows it.

**Other structures.** Chapter 5 continues with the *range tree*, which answers the same queries in O(log² *n* + *k*) time, or O(log *n* + *k*) with fractional cascading, at the price of O(*n* log *n*) storage; it will get a page of its own.

Step through the algorithm with the player. On the left, the plane shows the points (*v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, …), the vertical split lines in blue and the horizontal ones in red, and the query rectangle *R* dashed in purple; on the right, the tree draws split nodes as squares and leaves as circles. The node in focus and its region are orange, reported points and subtrees green, skipped regions and subtrees grey, and visited nodes have a thick outline. The **Node** panel shows the split line, depth and region of the current node, or the point of a leaf; **Query** shows how the child under test relates to *R* and counts the reported points, the visited nodes, the subtrees reported whole and the skipped ones; **Reported** lists the points found so far. The **Mode** menu shows both phases (*Build and query*), only the construction (*Build only*), or only the search on the finished tree (*Query only*). Drag the points, or the two corners of the query rectangle, to change the input; a dragged corner stops at the opposite one, and the player restarts. **New random input** draws new points and a new rectangle. The **Code** tab runs the same algorithms through `kdTree`, `rangeQuery` and `drawKdTree`; see the [documentation](/documentation) for the rest of the library.
