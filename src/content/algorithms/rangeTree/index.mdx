The [kd-tree](/algorithms/kdTree) answers an orthogonal range query, which asks for the points of a set *P* of *n* points in the plane that lie in an axis-parallel rectangle *R* = [*x* : *x′*] × [*y* : *y′*], with O(*n*) storage, but a query may take O(√*n* + *k*) time for *k* reported points. A *range tree* answers the same query in O(log² *n* + *k*) time; the price is O(*n* log *n*) storage.

This page follows section 5.3, *Range Trees*, of Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications, 3rd ed., by de Berg et al.

**From one dimension to two.** A 2-D range query consists of two 1-D queries, one on the *x*- and one on the *y*-coordinates of the points. In one dimension the points are stored in the leaves of a balanced binary search tree, and the query [*x* : *x′*] searches for *x* and for *x′* down to the *split node* where the two search paths part; the subtrees that hang off the paths below it, between them, lie in the range, and the query takes O(log *n* + *k*) time. This algorithm, 1DRangeQuery, is explained on the [kd-tree page](/algorithms/kdTree). For a node ν the *canonical subset* *P*(ν) is the set of points stored in the leaves below ν. So the 1-D query on *x* gives the points in the slab [*x* : *x′*] × (−∞ : +∞) as the disjoint union of O(log *n*) canonical subsets, the roots of the subtrees that hang off the two paths, and, possibly, the points in the two leaves where the paths end. Of each canonical subset only the points with a *y*-coordinate in [*y* : *y′*] are wanted, and that is a 1-D query on *y*, which is fast if the subset has a search tree on *y* of its own.

**Associated structures.** The range tree is a balanced binary search tree 𝒯 on the *x*-coordinates of the points, the *main tree*. Every node ν of 𝒯, leaves included, has a pointer to its *associated structure* 𝒯<sub>assoc</sub>(ν): a balanced binary search tree on the *y*-coordinates of *P*(ν), whose leaves store the points themselves, not just their *y*-coordinates. The book calls a structure in which the nodes of one tree have pointers to further trees a *multi-level data structure*; the main tree is its first level, the associated trees are the second.

**Building it.** **Build2DRangeTree**(*P*) first builds the associated structure on *P*. If *P* has a single point, it makes a leaf for it. Otherwise it splits *P* at the median *x*-coordinate *x*<sub>mid</sub> into *P*<sub>left</sub>, the points with an *x*-coordinate of at most *x*<sub>mid</sub>, and *P*<sub>right</sub>, the others, builds a subtree for each recursively, and makes a node that stores *x*<sub>mid</sub>, has the two subtrees as children and the tree built first as its associated structure. Built from unsorted points, an associated tree on *m* points takes O(*m* log *m*) time. The book avoids this: a balanced tree on points that are already sorted on *y* can be built bottom-up in linear time, so it sorts the points on *x* and on *y* once, beforehand, and splits both lists at every node; the time spent at a node of the main tree is then linear in the size of its canonical subset, and the whole construction takes as much time as the structure needs storage, O(*n* log *n*). The player simply sorts the points of each node, which is fast enough for a few dozen points.

**Why the storage is O(*n* log *n*).** A point *p* is stored only in the associated structures of the nodes on the path from the root of 𝒯 to the leaf of *p*. So the associated structures of all nodes at one depth of the main tree store every point at most once and together take O(*n*) storage, because a 1-D tree needs linear storage. The main tree has depth O(log *n*), so all associated structures together take O(*n* log *n*). The **Storage** panel counts the associated leaves: with a main tree of depth *h*, every point is in at most *h* + 1 associated trees, and in exactly *h* + 1 if *n* is a power of two, as in the *Square grid 4×4* preset (16 points, depth 4, 80 associated leaves). The ten points of the *Textbook example*, those of the kd-tree page scaled to this canvas, give a main tree of 19 nodes and depth 4 with 44 associated leaves, where the kd-tree stores each point once.

**The query.** **2DRangeQuery**(𝒯, [*x* : *x′*] × [*y* : *y′*]) first finds the split node ν<sub>split</sub> of the search paths for *x* and *x′* in the main tree; if it is a leaf, its point is checked against *R* and the query is done. Otherwise the query follows the path from the left child of ν<sub>split</sub> to *x*. At every node ν where the path goes left (*x* ≤ *x*<sub>ν</sub>), the right subtree of ν lies in the slab, and **1DRangeQuery**(𝒯<sub>assoc</sub>(rc(ν)), [*y* : *y′*]) reports its points with a *y*-coordinate in [*y* : *y′*]. In the same way the query follows the path from the right child of ν<sub>split</sub> to *x′* and queries the associated structures of the left subtrees that hang off it. The points in the two leaves where the paths end are checked against *R* directly. The rectangle is closed, so a point on its boundary is reported.

**Why the query takes O(log² *n* + *k*) time.** At each node on the two search paths the query spends constant time to decide where to go on, and it may call 1DRangeQuery, which takes O(log *n* + *k*<sub>ν</sub>) time when it reports *k*<sub>ν</sub> points. The paths have length O(log *n*), so there are O(log *n*) canonical subtrees and calls, and the canonical subsets are disjoint, so the *k*<sub>ν</sub> add up to *k*. In total the query takes O(log² *n* + *k*) time. In the player every associated query is one step: the *Thin vertical strip* preset, narrow in *x* and tall in *y*, makes four of them, and *Query covers everything* five.

**Equal coordinates.** As on the kd-tree page, the book removes the assumption that no two points share an *x*- or a *y*-coordinate with *composite numbers* (section 5.5): in the main tree the points are compared by (*x* | *y*), that is by *x* and then by *y*, in the associated trees by (*y* | *x*). The player does the same and orders coincident points by their number, so every input is valid; the *Square grid 4×4* preset, where four points share every *x* and every *y*, shows it.

**Further.** In *d* dimensions a range tree has a main tree on the first coordinate and, at every node, an associated (*d* − 1)-dimensional range tree on the other coordinates; it uses O(*n* log<sup>*d*−1</sup> *n*) storage and answers a query in O(log<sup>*d*</sup> *n* + *k*) time (section 5.4). *Fractional cascading* (section 5.6) brings the query time in the plane down to O(log *n* + *k*) with the same storage, by replacing the associated trees with sorted arrays linked to each other, so that the search for *y* is done only once; it will get a page of its own.

Step through the algorithm with the player. On the left, the plane shows the points (*p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, …) and the query rectangle *R*, dashed; on the right, the main tree on *x* is drawn above the associated tree on *y* of one of its nodes. The split nodes of the main tree are *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, … in pre-order, the leaves carry the labels of their points, and the associated tree shows its split values on *y*. In the build, the node in focus is orange, with its points and its split line, and the split lines of the nodes built before it are grey. In the query, the slab [*x* : *x′*] is shaded blue, the search paths are blue, canonical subtrees and reported points green, and the node in focus is orange, with its split line (a leaf in focus has its point ringed in orange); while the associated tree of a canonical subtree is queried, the subtree and its points are ringed in green, and its associated tree shows the two 1-D search paths in blue and the reported subtrees and points in green. The **Node** panel shows the *x*-range and the points of the current node, in the order of its associated leaves; **Result** lists the reported points and counts the canonical subtrees and associated queries; **Storage** gives the size of the whole range tree. The **Mode** menu shows both phases (*Build and query*), only the construction (*Build only*), or only the search on the finished tree (*Query only*). Drag the points, or the two corners of the query rectangle, to change the input; a dragged corner stops at the opposite one, and the player restarts. **New random input** draws new points and a new rectangle. The **Code** tab runs the same algorithms through `rangeTree`, `rangeTreeQuery` and `drawRangeTree`; see the [documentation](/documentation) for the rest of the library.
