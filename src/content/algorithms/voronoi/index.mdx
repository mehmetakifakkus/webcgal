Let *P* be a set of *n* points in the plane, called *sites*. The *Voronoi diagram* of *P* splits the plane into *n* *cells*, one per site: the cell of a site *p<sub>i</sub>* is the set of all points of the plane whose *nearest site* is *p<sub>i</sub>*, that is, the points closer to *p<sub>i</sub>* than to any other site. Each cell is convex: it is the intersection of the half-planes bounded by the perpendicular bisectors between *p<sub>i</sub>* and the other sites. A cell is unbounded exactly when its site lies on the convex hull of *P*. The boundaries between cells are the *Voronoi edges*, segments or rays of points equidistant from two sites, and the points where edges meet are the *Voronoi vertices*. Voronoi diagrams answer "which site is closest?" questions: the nearest post office, cell tower or hospital for every location.

This page follows chapter 7, *Voronoi Diagrams*, and section 9.2 of Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications, 3rd ed., by de Berg et al.

**Vertices and empty circles.** A Voronoi vertex is equidistant from (at least) three sites, so it is the centre of a circle through them. No site lies inside that circle: a site inside would be closer to the vertex than the three on the circle. Conversely, the centre of every such *empty circle* through three sites is a Voronoi vertex. A point on a Voronoi edge between *p<sub>i</sub>* and *p<sub>j</sub>* is likewise the centre of an empty circle through those two sites only.

**Duality with the Delaunay triangulation.** These empty circles are exactly the circumcircles of the [Delaunay Triangulation](/algorithms/delaunay): by Theorem 9.6, for points in general position (no four on a common circle), three sites are the vertices of a Delaunay triangle exactly when their circumcircle is empty, and two sites are joined by a Delaunay edge exactly when some empty circle passes through them. So the Voronoi diagram is the *dual* of the Delaunay triangulation. Every Delaunay triangle gives one Voronoi vertex, its circumcenter; vertices are merged when circumcenters coincide (see the degenerate cases below). Every Delaunay edge shared by two triangles gives the Voronoi edge between their two circumcenters, and every edge on the convex hull, which has only one triangle, gives a ray from the circumcenter of that triangle along the perpendicular bisector of the edge, away from the triangle. The cell of a site is bounded by the edges dual to its Delaunay edges, in counter-clockwise order.

**Why derive it from the Delaunay triangulation.** Once the triangulation is known, the diagram can be read off in one pass over its triangles and edges, in O(n) time since there are O(n) of each; with the randomized incremental Delaunay algorithm (expected O(n log n)), the whole construction takes expected O(n log n) time. It is also the simplest route when the triangulation is wanted anyway, and this page reuses the algorithm of the [Delaunay Triangulation](/algorithms/delaunay) page. Chapter 7 of the book instead builds the diagram directly with *Fortune's algorithm*, a sweep line that maintains a *beach line* of parabolic arcs, in O(n log n) time in the worst case.

**Degenerate cases.** When four or more sites lie on a common circle with no site inside, the Delaunay triangulation splits their polygon into several triangles with the same circumcenter. Their vertices are merged into one Voronoi vertex of higher degree, and the Delaunay edges between those triangles produce no Voronoi edge (the player marks them as skipped). The *Points on a circle* preset has a single vertex where all eight cells meet, and the *Square grid* preset has vertices of degree four. When all sites are collinear there is no Delaunay triangle and no Voronoi vertex: the diagram consists of the parallel perpendicular bisectors of consecutive sites, and every cell is an unbounded strip or half-plane.

Step through the construction with the player: first the Voronoi vertices, one per Delaunay triangle, with its circumcircle drawn dashed; then the edges and rays, one per Delaunay edge; then the cells. The **Vertices** panel shows which triangles each vertex comes from, **Edges** the Voronoi edge or ray of each Delaunay edge, and **Cells** the vertices around each cell, with ∞ for an unbounded one. Rays, bisector lines and unbounded cells are clipped to the canvas. The **Mode** menu shows the Delaunay triangulation underneath (*With Delaunay*) or hides it (*Voronoi only*); the steps are the same. Drag the points to change the input. Dragging a point onto another one shows a warning, and the point jumps back on release. The **Code** tab runs the same construction through `voronoi`, `drawVoronoi` and `voronoiCells`; see the [documentation](/documentation) for the rest of the library.
